Legio III Cyrenaica

This is a secondary Legion who attacked Jerusalem in 70AD.

Legio III Cyrenaica (Third Legion "Cyrenaica"): Based in Egypt (garrisoned outside Alexandria), this legion dispatched a vexillation to Judea to serve alongside Titus during the operations against Jerusalem. Legio III Cyrenaica

Like most Roman legions during the late Republic and Imperial era, Legio III Cyrenaica did not consist of a single nationality. Its composition shifted over time based on where it was raised, where it was stationed, and how the Roman military recruited.

The main national and ethnic groups that made up the legion throughout its history included:

  1. Romans and Italians (Late Republic – Early Principate)

    When the legion was raised around 36BCE (likely by Mark Antony or Marcus Aemilius Lepidus), its officer corps and initial core legionaries were Italic Romans. During the early Roman Empire, citizens of Italian origin held most command positions and formed a major part of new recruits.

  2. Cyrenaicans / Libyans (Founding Recruitment)

    The legion received its surname Cyrenaica because it was raised or first garrisoned in Cyrenaica (modern-day eastern coastal Libya). Early recruits included local Hellenized citizens and settlers from this North African region.

  3. Greeks and Hellenized Egyptians (1st Century BCE – 1st Century CE)

    After Octavian defeated Antony and annexed Egypt in 30BCE, Legio III Cyrenaica was garrisoned near Alexandria and Upper Egypt for over a century.

    • Roman legionaries were legally required to be Roman citizens; however, in Egypt, local Ptolemaic Greeks and Graeco-Egyptians were frequently granted citizenship upon enlistment to fill the ranks.
    • Epigraphic and papyrological evidence from Egypt shows that many soldiers had Greek names or Greek-Egyptian heritage.
  4. Syrians, Arabians, and Levantines (2nd Century CE – 5th Century CE)

    In 106 CE, Emperor Trajan annexed the Nabataean Kingdom, creating the province of Arabia Petraea. Legio III Cyrenaica was relocated to its new permanent base at Bostra (modern-day Bosra, Syria / southern Levant).

    • Once permanently stationed in the Levant, local recruitment took over completely. The vast majority of recruits were local Syrians, Nabataeans, and Arabs from the surrounding regions.
    • The legion retained its regional identity in Bostra until its final records in the early 5th century CE.
