Legio XXII Deiotariana

This is a secondary Legion who attacked Jerusalem in 70AD.

Legio XXII Deiotariana (Twenty-Second Legion "Deiotariana"): Also stationed in Egypt alongside the Third Legion, it sent a detachment to assist with siege operations and logistical support. Legio XXII Deiotariana

Legio XXII Deiotariana was unique among Roman legions because it began as a royal native army before being fully integrated into the Roman military system. Its composition evolved over time to include several distinct groups:

  1. Galatians (Celts of Asia Minor)

    The original foundation and core ethnic group of the legion were Galatians—a Celtic people (specifically tribes such as the Tolistobogii) living in central Anatolia (modern-day Turkey). Around 48–47 BC, King Deiotarus of Galatia raised a 12,000-man army trained and equipped in the Roman style. After sustaining severe losses fighting Pharnaces II of Pontus, the surviving troops were organized into a single Roman-style unit that later became Legio XXII Deiotariana.

  2. Anatolians and Hellenistic Greeks (Asia Minor)

    During its early decades as an auxiliary and allied legion, replacement recruits continued to be drawn primarily from Galatia and neighboring regions in Asia Minor.

  3. Greco-Egyptians and Local Levantine Recruits When Rome annexed Galatia in 25 BC, the legion was officially incorporated into the Imperial Roman Army as the 22nd Legion and stationed at Alexandria, Egypt. Over more than a century of deployment in Egypt, routine replenishment of troops shifted to local recruitment:
    • Hellenized Greeks and native Egyptians: Recruited locally in Egypt (who were granted Roman citizenship upon enlistment or served as enfranchised provincials).
    • Syrians and Levantines: Recruits drawn from nearby Roman provinces in the Eastern Mediterranean.
  4. Italic Romans and Provincial Citizens (Officers)

    Commanders, centurions, and senior officers were predominantly Roman citizens of Italic origin or from long-established Roman colonies across the Empire. Because Egypt was a imperial domain forbidden to senators, the legion was uniquely commanded by an equestrian prefect (praefectus legionis) rather than a senatorial legate.
